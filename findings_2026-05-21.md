## 3) Findings

### 3.1 Mode ordering: ID >> F₉ > HW ≈ HD at low sigma; F₉ collapses with noise

The four modes occupy very different operating regimes:

- **ID** achieves ~49% byte-level SR and 0.90–0.93 SASCA AUC at every tested sigma.
  Its performance is essentially flat across the entire sweep (see §3.3).

- **F₉** starts strongest (among the three non-trivial modes) at low noise:
  SASCA AUC=1.00 at σ=0.1, well above HW (0.44) and HD (0.36). But F₉ degrades
  dramatically: AUC drops to 0.05 by σ=4.0, below both HW (0.09) and HD (0.08).
  The high-noise F₉ regime is worse than HW/HD.

- **HW and HD** are nearly indistinguishable throughout the sweep. HW is
  consistently 5–15% higher in SASCA AUC, which is plausible (see §3.4).
  Both degrade gradually and predictably with sigma.

The crossover between F₉ and HW/HD in SASCA AUC occurs between σ=1.5 and σ=2.0.
Below that crossover F₉ is the best classical leakage model; above it HW or HD
would be a better simulator choice.

### 3.2 F₉ family structure: structural crosstalk floors, not noise floors

The F₉ average SR of 25.7% at σ=0.1 masks a **36× spread across families**
(73% for A vs 2.1% for D). This is entirely structural, not noise-related —
it reflects how many key bytes contribute to each intermediate value:

- **Family A** (state after rho+pi, before chi): bytes have undergone linear theta
  diffusion and rho/pi permutation but not chi. In the F₉ model each 32-bit word
  leaks all its bits via random coefficients. The target byte contributes 4 of the
  32 bits (12.5%) with coherent signal; the other 28 bits are crosstalk from the
  same lane. At low noise the 4-bit F₉ signal is easily resolved → SR=73%.

- **Family B** (after chi): chi mixes three consecutive x-lane values non-linearly.
  Each output bit now depends on 3 A-lanes. The target byte's bits are diluted by
  a wider interference footprint → SR=20%.

- **Family C** (column parities, CB[x] = XOR of 5 lanes): each C lane mixes 5 full
  state lanes = 40 bytes. The target byte contributes 4 bits to a 320-bit pool.
  Almost entirely crosstalk at the F₉ level → SR=9%.

- **Family D** (theta correction, DB[x] derived from CB[(x+4)%5] and CB[(x+1)%5]):
  each D lane depends on 2 column parities = 10 state lanes = 80 bytes. The target
  byte is buried in ~640-bit interference → SR=2%, essentially random.

**Families C and D cannot be recovered from F₉ leakage regardless of noise level.**
SASCA must reconstruct their beliefs entirely through constraint propagation from
A and B likelihoods. This is a fundamental property of the F₉ model applied to
post-theta intermediates, not a parameter-tuning issue.

The implication for paperscale runs: the F₉ sigma-sweep average SR will always be
dragged down by C and D families. Evaluating F₉ quality by the overall mean SR is
misleading; the A-family SR (and SASCA AUC) is the relevant metric.

### 3.3 ID noise insensitivity: ICS selection is sigma-invariant up to σ≈6

The ID template GE is **completely flat** across σ=0.1–4.0 (51.0 ± 0.1).
HW GE rises monotonically from 44 to 97 over the same range. The contrast is
decisive: ID is training-data-limited, HW is noise-limited.

The reason is the ICS selection threshold. The pipeline selects samples with
correlation r > 0.09. For ID (identity/bit-level) leakage, the bit-level
correlation is:

```
r_ID(σ) = sqrt(Var_bit / (Var_bit + σ²)) = sqrt(0.25 / (0.25 + σ²))
```

Values:

| σ | r_ID | Selected? |
|---|------|----------|
| 0.1 | 0.98 | yes |
| 1.0 | 0.45 | yes |
| 4.0 | 0.12 | yes |
| 5.5 | 0.09 | barely |
| 6.0 | 0.08 | **no** |

The ICS selection threshold is not crossed until σ≈6. Across the entire σ=0.1–4.0
sweep, **the exact same ICS points are selected**, the LDA sees the same feature set,
and the resulting templates are effectively identical. The sigma variable has been
factored out of the ID pipeline entirely.

The SR plateau of ≈49% corresponds to recovering **7 out of 8 bits** of each byte
(SR ≈ 1/2^1 = 50%). The 8th bit narrowly fails the ICS threshold for some fraction
of bytes, leaving one unknown bit. The GE of ~51 is consistent with this interpretation
(bimodal distribution: 49% correct at rank 1, 51% failure concentrated at rank ~100).

**Conclusion:** The ID sigma sweep at smoke scale is not a sigma sweep — it is the
same run repeated nine times. Real noise sensitivity for ID mode requires either:
(a) paperscale training data (so training-data effects shrink and noise effects
become visible), or (b) sigma > 5.5 where ICS points start dropping out.
The results in §2.1–2.3 for ID should not be interpreted as noise-sensitivity data.

### 3.4 HW ≈ HD throughout; small but real HD deficit

HW and HD track each other closely in template SR, GE, and SASCA AUC across all
sigma. The small but consistent gap (HW SASCA AUC ~10–25% higher than HD) is
plausible: HD = HW(byte XOR byte_prev) depends on two consecutive state values,
adding one extra unknown. The extra unknown makes byte-level template discrimination
marginally harder and gives BP weaker initial likelihoods.

Neither mode shows the structural family variation seen in F₉ — this is expected
since HW and HD map many bytes to the same Hamming weight, making the family
membership of a byte irrelevant to its discriminability.

### 3.5 Extra Keccak rounds help F₉ and ID, not HW/HD

SASCA 4R AUC vs 2R AUC:

| Mode | 2R AUC at σ=0.5 | 4R AUC at σ=0.5 | Gain |
|------|----------------|----------------|------|
| HW | 0.32 | 0.27 | −0.05 (negligible) |
| HD | 0.29 | 0.25 | −0.04 (negligible) |
| ID | 0.93 | 1.00 | +0.07 |
| F₉ | 0.76 | 0.97 | **+0.21** |

For F₉, going from 2R to 4R nearly closes the gap with ID at σ=0.5. This is because
F₉ templates per byte are weak enough that BP needs more leakage rounds to converge.
ID is already close to saturated at 2R. HW/HD gain nothing from extra rounds because
BP cannot exploit weak per-byte likelihoods regardless of round count.

## 4) Implications

### 4.1 F₉ is the best model at low noise, worst at high noise

The crossover at σ≈1.5–2.0 means the choice of leakage model matters for the
sigma regime being simulated. For real hardware characterisation at low noise (σ<1.5),
F₉ gives the best attack success. Above σ≈2.0, F₉ is no longer the appropriate model.

### 4.2 ID sigma results cannot be used for noise sensitivity

If the purpose of the ID sweep was to characterise how the ID attack degrades with
noise, the results are invalid at smoke scale. A paperscale ID sigma sweep (or a
sweep extending to σ=6–8) is needed to observe real degradation.

### 4.3 F₉ SASCA relies on A and B families to bootstrap BP for C and D

Since families C and D contribute near-random beliefs under F₉, the full-system SASCA
success depends on A-family templates being strong enough to let BP reconstruct C/D
through the Keccak factor graph. At σ>2.5 where A-family F₉ SR drops below ~1%,
BP has almost nothing to propagate and the attack fails, consistent with the AUC
collapse observed in §2.3.

## 5) Infrastructure: OOM Kill — May 21 Paperscale Run Interruption (2026-05-22)

### 5.1 What happened

All 27+ pipeline processes on the IDP remote died simultaneously between 10:41–10:51 AM
on May 21. Sandboxes affected: all 7 hd R2 runs, all 9 hw R2 runs, all 9 id R2 runs,
3 f9-4c R2 runs, 2 hd training runs, 3 f9-4a simulation processes.

The kill correlated superficially with the launch of f9-4a simulations (launched 09:13,
processes died ~10:41). This is the second such incident.

### 5.2 Root cause: OOM killer, 28 concurrent R2 detection processes

`sar` data from `/var/log/sysstat/sa21` gives the memory timeline:

| Time | Mem used | Commit ratio | State |
|------|----------|-------------|-------|
| 10:10 AM | 35.3% | 47.7% | hw+id barely in R2 |
| 10:20 AM | 40.6% | 56.2% | hw+id R2 processes loading |
| 10:30 AM | 63.0% | **89.5%** | hw+id fully loaded — steep jump |
| 10:40 AM | 81.4% | **106.7%** | **OOM threshold crossed** |
| 10:50 AM | 94.0% | **118.0%** | OOM killer still firing |
| 10:10 PM | 0.34% | 0.21% | ~993 GB freed — everything dead |

The Linux overcommit limit is physical RAM + swap×50% ≈ 1065 GB on this machine.
Commit ratio exceeding 100% triggers the OOM killer. Between 10:20 and 10:30 AM,
the 18 hw+id R2 detection processes loaded their numpy trace arrays into memory,
committing another ~350 GB of virtual address space and pushing the system over the edge.

Concurrent R2 processes at the time of OOM:
- 7 hd (ETA had been 07:00 AM; 7/9 were still in R2 at 09:13 when hw+id launched)
- 9 hw (entered R2 at ~10:23)
- 9 id (entered R2 at ~10:25)
- 3 f9-4c (running since May 19)

**Total: 28 concurrent R2 processes.** The plan assumed a ≤24 peak; the actual peak
was 28 because hd R2 ran 2–3h longer than estimated for the slower-SNR sigmas.

### 5.3 The f9 simulation was a red herring (mostly)

The f9-4a simulation caused a visible memory spike at 09:20 AM (kbmemfree dropped to
5.6 GB, kbdirty hit 8.7 GB from write-back storm as 3 simulations wrote trace data
concurrently). However, by 10:10 AM memory had recovered to normal levels. The
simulation was not running heavy computation at 10:40 — it was only in the TR phase
(sequential trace writing, ~1 CPU core per run). The OOM was overwhelmingly driven by
the R2 process count, not the simulation.

The coincidence arises because hw+id were launched simultaneously with f9-4a (09:13),
so everything entered its heaviest memory phase together ~1h later.

### 5.4 Why the hd ETA was wrong

The relaunch plan estimated hd R2 done by 07:00 AM (ETA from 9-way parallelism at
7.2h/run). In practice, only 2/9 hd runs (σ=2.5 and σ=3.5) cleared R2 by early
morning; the remaining 7 ran until ~10:30+ AM. The slower runs are the low-noise
sigmas (σ=0.1–1.5) where more ICS are selected, detection processes more bits, and
each `detect()` call is proportionally more expensive. The 7.2h/run average masked
this variance.

### 5.5 Operational fix

The safe concurrent R2 limit for this machine is **≤18 processes** (not 24).
At 18 concurrent runs of paperscale R2 detection, commit ratio stays below 90%
with adequate headroom. Any new batch should only launch after enough existing
R2 runs have completed to keep the total at or below 18.

The trigger for hw+id should have been "all 9 hd runs show `DONE: 0002 detection R2`",
not a time estimate. Always verify via log grep before launching the next batch.
